About the Public Sector Data Quality Commons

A free, open, shared place where anyone working with public sector data can check it, improve it, learn how, and help each other, so that public services and decisions rest on data people can trust.

The Commons began as the data quality work behind the UK data asset management (DAM) policy and the National Data Library (NDL). It is now general: the same toolkit and knowledge work for any data quality purpose, in any organisation, with sections specific to each framework, policy, sector and platform.

Background

Government runs on data: benefits, health care, schools, planning and statistics all depend on records being complete, accurate and up to date. The Government Data Quality Framework (2020) set out principles and dimensions for managing quality. In 2026 the data asset management policy asked departments to identify their critical data assets and keep a data quality action plan, or equivalent, for each one, and the National Data Library began bringing high-value data together in one place.

The Commons started as the practical data quality work behind those two developments. It is now general: the same toolkit and knowledge work for any public body, sector or nation.

Why it exists: the problem it solves

  • Everyone starts from scratch. Teams across government build their own rule engines and spreadsheets, so results can't be compared and effort is duplicated.
  • Frameworks don't talk to each other. UK, devolved, sector and international frameworks use different words for the same ideas, which makes shared data hard to judge.
  • Quality is rarely published. People who reuse data often can't see how good it is, or which groups it leaves out.
  • Skills and time are thin. Data quality sits inside many professions' frameworks but is a full-time job for very few people.
  • Tools can be costly. Smaller bodies, local government and the voluntary sector often have no budget for commercial platforms.

Who it is for

No coding needed. Every task has a no-install route in the browser, and your data never leaves your device unless you choose to share something.

Purpose and aims

One place

Bring frameworks, standards, tools, rules, training and people for data quality into one shared front door, with signposts to the official sources.

Practical by default

Everything leads to something you can do today: run a check, write a rule, prioritise an issue, report a baseline.

Open and portable

Open standards (W3C DQV, DCAT, CSV, YAML), MIT code and OGL content. Rules move between tools; nothing locks you in.

Fair and inclusive

Check who data leaves out, design for people who use assistive technology, and give every nation and sector a place.

Goals and desired outcomes

How the work is meant to lead to change (from the theory of change):

  1. InputsPublished guidance and standards, open code from across government, and the Commons community.
  2. ActivitiesBuild an open toolkit and knowledge base; organisations check data, learn by role and share what works.
  3. OutputsMeasured baselines, rules that move between tools, translated results across frameworks, case studies.
  4. Short-term outcomesA common language and method; fewer duplicated tools; inclusion checks routine for personal data.
  5. Medium-term outcomesQuality published alongside data; issues fixed at source; skills spread across professions.
  6. ImpactPublic services and decisions rest on data people can trust, including for the groups data often leaves out.

Objectives for the first year

Measure what matters

Help organisations set a measured baseline for their critical data assets, using the government's issues framework.

Measure: assets with a baseline (opt-in reporting).

Make frameworks usable

Map 30 frameworks and standards to one set of dimensions and checks, so a result in one translates into another.

Measure: frameworks verified and mapped; packs in use.

Build skills

Offer role-based learning from Awareness to Expert, linked to the Government Digital and Data (GDaD) capability framework and the other government professions' frameworks (see Data quality across the professions).

Measure: modules completed; confidence from the feedback survey.

Share what works

Grow a moderated library of case studies, rules and lessons from across sectors and nations.

Measure: contributions, and the share from outside central government.

Use AI safely

Show how to get value from AI for data quality, with evaluation and assurance before anything is reused.

Measure: AI components evaluated and recorded under ATRS.

Include everyone

Meet WCAG 2.2 AA, offer a Welsh version, and make inclusion checks routine for personal data.

Measure: open accessibility issues and their age.

Priorities

  1. Critical data first. Work that helps the most important data, used by the most people, comes first.
  2. Accessibility and inclusion. When priorities tie, the change that helps people often left out wins.
  3. Reuse before build. Signpost official guidance and existing tools; build only where there is a gap.
  4. Evidence over opinion. Every claim links to a source, and anything not yet checked says so.
  5. Human decisions. AI drafts and explains; people decide.

What the Commons is not

It is a prototype public good service, not an official government product, and it doesn't replace official guidance. Where an official source exists (for example the Government Data Quality Framework, the DAM policy or NHS England's DQMI), we link to it first. Anything we haven't checked against the source is marked "partly verified".
